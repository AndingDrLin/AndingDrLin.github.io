4 9.1 Electronic Packaging Technology The electronic packaging has six functions: Signal distribution , involving topological and electromagnetic considerations; Power distribution , involving electromagnetic, structural, and material aspects; Heat dissipation (thermal management), involving structural and mate- rial considerations; Support and protection (mechanical, chemical, and electromagnetic) components and interconnections from the hostile environments; Design (for performance, environment, manufacture ability, and reliability) at the front end and system test at the final stage prior to the system shipment; System testing that involves every aspect of system reliabilities.

CSP, BGA. WLA 11 CSP: An improvement in packaging efficiency beyond BGAs is achieved by CSP. Most CSPs use substrate (interposer) or metal layer to redistribute the very fine-pith (as small as 0.075 mm) peripheral pads on the chip to a much larger pitch (1 mm, 0.8 mm, 0.75 mm, and 0.5 mm) area array pads on the PWB. WLP is the technology employed for the fabrication of ICs while still part of the wafer, in contrast to the more conventional method of slicing the wafer into individual circuits (dice) and then packaging them. This technique has grown rapidly in popularity in the IC industry due to advantages in terms of component size as well as production time and cost. WLP is essentially a type of chip-scale package (CSP) technology, since the resulting package is practically of the same size as the die.

Fan-in and Fan-out 14 Fan-In Wafer-Level Package (FI-WLP) refers to the technology of packaging an integrated circuit (IC) at the wafer level, instead of the traditional process of assembling individual dies into packages after dicing them from a wafer . FI-WLP technology is an extension of the wafer fabrication process and uses the traditional fabrication processes and tools. Fan-Out Wafer-Level Package (FO-WLP) is an enhancement of standard WLPs, enabling a greater number of I/O connections. This package involves dicing chips from a silicon wafer, precisely positioning the known-good- die on a “reconstituted” or “carrier” wafer / panel, which is then molded .

Ball Grid Array 18 Ball Grid Array (BGA) Ball grid arrays ( see Fig. 2-3 ), with 1.27-mm pitch (distance between adjacent ball centers ) and finer pitch versions with 1- and 0.8-mm pitches, are the only choice for packages with higher than 300 I/O counts, replacing leaded packages such as the quad flat pack (QFP). BGA s provide improved electrical and thermal performance, more effective manufacturing, and ease-of-handling compared to conventional surface mount (SMT ) leaded parts. Finer pitch area array packages ( FPBGA ), also known as CSPs , are further miniaturized versions of BGA s, or smaller configurations of leaded and leadless packages with features generally less than 0.8-mm pitches.

Challenging 2D Packaging and Advancement of 3D Packaging 19 Challenges in Traditional 2D Packaging Interconnect Bottlenecks: As transistor sizes shrink (following Moore’s Law), the number of interconnects increases, leading to signal delays and power inefficiencies due to long wiring distances. Power and Thermal Issues: Higher power densities in advanced nodes lead to increased heat dissipation challenges. Power delivery becomes inefficient due to longer traces. Form Factor Constraints: 2D scaling is reaching physical limits, making further miniaturization difficult. Larger packages are needed for multi-chip solutions, increasing cost. Bandwidth Limitations: Data transfer between chips is constrained by the limited number of I/ Os in 2D configurations. Memory bandwidth (e.g., between processors and DRAM) becomes a bottleneck (the "memory wall" problem).

--- SLIDE 20 ---
Challenging 2D Packaging and Advancement of 3D Packaging 20 3D Stacking Technologies Through-Silicon Vias (TSVs): Vertical electrical connections that pass-through silicon dies, enabling direct stacking. Hybrid Bonding: Advanced bonding technique that allows ultra-fine pitch interconnects (<10µm). Chiplet -Based Designs: Disaggregates large monolithic dies into smaller " chiplets " that can be stacked or placed side-by-side. Types of 3D Packaging 3D IC Stacking (Die-on-Die): Logic-on-logic or memory-on-logic stacking (e.g., HBM on GPUs). 2.5D Interposer-Based Packaging: Dies placed side-by-side on a silicon interposer with TSVs (e.g., NVIDIA’s CoWoS ). Fan-Out Wafer-Level Packaging (FOWLP): Dies embedded in epoxy mold with redistribution layers (RDLs) for high-density interconnects.

Impact and Mitigate of M ismatched CTE in Microelectronics P ackaging 21 Mismatched CTE causes mechanical stress during thermal cycling (e.g., power cycling, reflow soldering, environmental changes), leading to reliability issues. A. Thermo-Mechanical Stress & Warpage Die Cracking/Delamination: Silicon dies bonded to substrates (e.g., flip-chip BGA) experience bending stress. S tress can crack brittle silicon or delaminate interfaces Package Warpage: Uneven expansion causes warping, leading to misalignment in assembly (e.g., solder joint failures). B. Solder Joint Fatigue Crack Formation: Repeated thermal cycling induces fatigue in solder balls (e.g., in BGA packages). Electrical Failures: Cracked solder joints increase resistance, leading to intermittent or open connections .

--- SLIDE 22 ---
Impact and Mitigate of M ismatched CTE in Microelectronics P ackaging 22 C. Underfill & Interconnect Reliability Underfill Cracking: High-CTE underfill materials can induce stress on low-CTE silicon. Through-Silicon Via (TSV) Reliability: In 3D ICs, TSVs experience stress due to Si-Cu CTE mismatch, risking via cracking. Mitigation Strategies for CTE Mismatch Material Selection & Design Low-CTE Substrates: Use ceramic ( Al₂O ₃, ~6–8 ppm/°C) or advanced organic substrates with adjusted CTE. Example: Ajinomoto Build-up Film (ABF) for better CTE matching. Underfill Optimization: CTE-Tailored Underfill: Modified epoxy with fillers (e.g., silica) to reduce CTE (~10–25 ppm/°C). Corner- vs. Full-Underfill: Selective application to minimize stress. Compliant Interconnects: Copper Pillars with Solder Cap: More flexible than traditional solder bumps. Polymer-Based Interposers: Reduces stress in 2.5D/3D packages.

Heat F low Across S olid I nterfaces: Conduction, Convection, and Radiation 23 Challenges of Heat Conduction Across Solid Interfaces A. Thermal Contact Resistance (TCR) Cause: Microscopic air gaps or surface roughness (~µm-scale) reduce effective contact area. Impact: High interfacial temperature drop (e.g., CPU-heatsink interfaces), and degrades thermal performance in electronics packaging. Mitigation: Thermal Interface Materials (TIMs): Greases, gels, phase-change materials (PCMs), or metal-based TIMs (e.g., indium, graphene). Surface Polishing & Flatness Control: Reduces air gaps. B. Material Mismatch (CTE & Thermal Conductivity) Cause: Different thermal expansion coefficients (CTE) and conductivities (k) between materials. Impact: Warping or delamination under thermal cycling (e.g., Si die to Cu heat spreader). Mitigation: Graded CTE interlayers (e.g., tungsten-copper composites). Anisotropic thermal conductors (e.g., graphite sheets).

--- SLIDE 24 ---
Heat F low Across S olid I nterfaces: Conduction, Convection, and Radiation 24 Challenges of Heat Conduction Across Solid Interfaces C. Phonon Scattering at Nanoscale Interfaces Cause: In nanostructured materials (e.g., Si-Ge superlattices), phonon scattering reduces thermal conductivity. Impact: Limits heat dissipation in advanced semiconductor devices. Mitigation: Phonon engineering (e.g., aligned nanowires, metamaterials). Challenges of Heat Convection Across Solid-Fluid Interfaces Key Challenges: A. Boundary Layer Resistance Cause: A stagnant fluid layer forms near the solid surface, acting as insulation. Impact: Reduces cooling efficiency (e.g., in heat sinks). Mitigation: Turbulence promoters (fins, microchannels). High-velocity flow (forced convection).

--- SLIDE 25 ---
Heat F low Across S olid I nterfaces: Conduction, Convection, and Radiation 25 Challenges of Heat Conduction Across Solid Interfaces B. Non-Uniform Flow Distribution Cause: Poor fluid dynamics (e.g., dead zones in heat exchangers). Impact: Hotspots in electronics cooling. Mitigation: Optimized fin/pin layouts (e.g., staggered vs. inline). Jet impingement cooling (direct high-speed fluid jets). C. Phase Change Limitations (Boiling/Condensation) Cause : Bubble formation (nucleate boiling) can lead to dry-out or film boiling (critical heat flux). Impact: Sudden temperature spikes in high-power electronics. Mitigation: Microporous surfaces (enhance nucleation). Dielectric fluids (for immersion cooling).

--- SLIDE 26 ---
Heat F low Across S olid I nterfaces: Conduction, Convection, and Radiation 26 Challenged of Heat Radiation Across Solid Interfaces Radiation is electromagnetic wave-based heat transfer (no medium required). Key Challenges: A. Low Emissivity of Metals Cause: Metals (e.g., Al, Cu) reflect most IR radiation. Impact: Poor radiative cooling in electronics. Mitigation: High-emissivity coatings (e.g., anodized Al, carbon black). B. View Factor & Shadowing Cause: Geometric obstructions reduce radiative exchange. Impact: Inefficient heat rejection in spacecraft/electronics. Mitigation: Optimized radiator designs (angled fins, selective emitters). Emerging Solutions Hybrid Cooling: Combining conduction (heat pipes) + convection (liquid cooling) + radiation (IR coatings). Nanostructured TIMs: Graphene, boron nitride for ultra-low TCR. AI-Optimized Heat Sinks: Machine learning for fluid/thermal design.

H eterogeneous I ntegration in Modern E lectrical P ackaging D esign 27 Introduction to Heterogeneous Integration (HI) Heterogeneous Integration (HI) refers to the advanced packaging technique of combining multiple dissimilar chips (e.g., processors, memory, sensors, RF, and analog components) into a single system. Unlike traditional monolithic ICs, HI enables: Higher performance (shorter interconnects, lower latency). Better power efficiency (optimized partitioning). Smaller form factor (3D stacking, chiplets ). Cost-effective scaling (mix-and-match process nodes). Challenges in Heterogeneous Integration A. Thermal Management Problem: 3D stacking increases power density, leading to hotspots. Solution: Microfluidic cooling, thermal vias, and advanced TIMs. B. Signal Integrity & Power Delivery Problem: High-speed signals between chiplets suffer from crosstalk and loss. Solution: Co-design of interconnects, low-loss dielectrics. C. Testing & Known-Good-Die (KGD) Problem: Ensuring each chiplet works before assembly. Solution: Built-in self-test (BIST), advanced probe testing.

Advanced Packaging Technologies, Like 2.5D and 3D 28 2.5D Packaging: Bridging the Gap Between 2D and 3D What is 2.5D Packaging? Dies are placed side-by-side on an interposer (silicon, organic, or glass) that provides high-density interconnects. No direct stacking (hence "2.5D" – more than 2D but not fully 3D). Advantages of 2.5D ✅ High bandwidth (short interconnects via interposer). ✅ Better thermal management (compared to full 3D stacking). ✅ Supports heterogeneous integration (e.g., CPU + HBM memory). Challenges ❌ Cost (silicon interposers are expensive). ❌ Thermal issues (heat buildup in dense interconnects).

--- SLIDE 29 ---
Advanced Packaging Technologies, Like 2.5D and 3D 29 What is 3D Packaging? Dies are stacked vertically (e.g., memory-on-logic) using hybrid bonding, microbumps , or TSVs . Enables ultra-short interconnects (microns vs. millimeters in 2D). Advantages of 3D ✅ Highest performance (shortest possible interconnects). ✅ Smallest footprint (ideal for mobile/AI chips). ✅ Power efficiency (reduced parasitic capacitance). D. Challenges ❌ Thermal issues (heat trapped between layers). ❌ Complex manufacturing (alignment, bonding yield). ❌ Testing difficulties (hard to probe stacked dies).
